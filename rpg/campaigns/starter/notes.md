## The Coal Pit

The Setup

Players take the drainage job expecting routine work. The job posting mentioned “previous crew found some unstable support beams” - what it didn’t mention is that the previous crew fled in terror, spreading wild tales about a “demon with burning eyes.”

The Rival Crew Situation

The Desperate Locals: The Harrow family - Joren (father), Mila (teenage daughter), and old Beck (Mila’s grandfather who knows mining)

• Lost their farm when their village was evacuated due to war proximity

• Arrived at the Bulwark a day after the players got the job

• Claim they had a “handshake agreement” with Sergeant Maddox

• Motivations: Genuinely desperate, have been sleeping rough, winter is coming

• Initial Approach: Try reasoning, then pleading, then veiled threats about “accidents happen in mines”

The Beast Problem Develops

Day 1: Players hear the stories

• Locals whisper about the “coal demon”

• Previous workers describe “eyes like burning coals” and an “unholy roar”

• Some talk about finding claw marks on the mine supports

Day 2-3: First encounters

• Deep growling from the flooded sections when they start pumping

• Glimpses of a large, completely black shape moving in the shadows

• Strong animal musk smell mixed with coal dust

• The Reality: Mama bear (about 6 months trapped, desperate, starving) with two half-grown cubs

The Sabotage Element

Night 1: Beck suggests the Harrows “convince” the players to share the job

• Equipment moved or hidden (not damaged yet - just intimidation)

• Drainage hoses repositioned so they pump into the wrong areas

• Investigation reveals: Footprints, disturbed equipment setup

Night 2-3: Escalation if players don’t compromise

• Holes poked in drainage hoses

• Support beams deliberately loosened (creating real cave-in danger)

• Beck’s desperation: “If we can’t work, no one can”

Multiple Solutions for Each Problem

For the Bear Crisis:

1. Food Solution: Provide food to calm the bear while figuring out escape route

2. Engineering Solution: Build ramps or pulley systems to help them escape

3. Negotiation: Work with fortress to get proper help (ropes, manpower, maybe a ranger)

4. Waiting Game: Drain enough water to open natural escape routes

For the Rival Crew:

1. Compromise: Split the job and payment

2. Competition: Challenge them to prove who deserves the work

3. Investigation: Prove they have no legitimate claim

4. Sympathy: Help them find other work at the fortress

5. Authority: Report them to Sergeant Maddox

For the Sabotage:

1. Prevention: Set watches, check equipment daily

2. Detection: Track the saboteurs, gather evidence

3. Confrontation: Face the Harrows directly about the sabotage

4. Security: Sleep on-site or hide equipment elsewhere

Complications & Consequences

• Time Pressure: Job needs finishing before winter storms

• Equipment Costs: Sabotaged equipment comes out of their pay

• Reputation: How they handle the situation affects future job opportunities

• Safety: Real risk of cave-ins from both unstable supports and sabotage

• Moral Choices: The Harrows aren’t evil, just desperate

Potential Outcomes

The adventure succeeds when players find solutions that address the human need (Harrow family), animal welfare (bear family), and practical completion of their job. The best outcomes involve creative problem-solving rather than violence.

This gives level 1 characters real stakes, multiple problems to solve, and various approaches that don’t require high-level abilities or magic - just clever thinking and good roleplay.

## Road Work

The Ghost of the Quarry Road

How It Appears
	•	While clearing debris, the crew disturbs a collapsed waystation or cairn where construction workers died in a rockslide decades ago.
	•	At dusk, when the mountains grow quiet, one figure remains — the spirit of a road worker still bound to the site.
	•	He is not violent at first: pale, dust-covered, wielding phantom tools, endlessly chipping at the rocks as if still on shift.

⸻

Ghost Behavior
	•	Suspicious, Not Hostile: He questions the crew in a flat, weary voice: “Are you taking our shifts?” or “Did the overseer send you?”
	•	Bound to Duty: He obsesses over roadwork quotas, muttering about pay withheld, food shortages, or beatings.
	•	Resentment: If the crew disturbs cairns, bones, or refuses to “help with the work,” his tone sharpens. Only then might he become hostile — throwing phantom stones or causing small rockslides.

⸻

Complications the Ghost Creates
	1.	Work Interference
	•	He “helps” by moving phantom stones, confusing real work.
	•	If ignored, he sabotages progress: rocks shift strangely, tools go missing, ropes fray.
	2.	Memory Fragment
	•	He mutters about unsafe blasting powder buried in the slope (true) or a hidden supply cache his crew stashed.
	•	Following his hints can uncover dangerous materials or useful salvage.
	3.	Unfinished Business
	•	He wants his crew’s cairn properly marked, or his name (half-carved in rock) finished.
	•	He might demand the players carry word back to the Bulwark that the workers were never paid.

⸻

Solutions
	•	Respectful Burial: Bury or mark bones, repair the cairn, say prayers — this calms him.
	•	Negotiation: Persuade him his “shift is over,” or that the road is being maintained in his honor.
	•	Appeasement: Work alongside him for a while (slowing progress) until he fades.
	•	Hostile Resolution: Drive him off with steel or spells if patience runs out, though this feels cruel and invites accidents.

⸻

Outcomes
	•	Peaceful Resolution: Ghost fades, the road is safer, and the Authority may be impressed (or annoyed at delays).
	•	Practical Exploitation: Following his memories reveals hidden salvage (iron, tools, blasting powder) — but risks a dangerous landslide.
	•	Violent End: Destroying the ghost earns pay but leaves the sense of injustice unresolved. Reputation among superstitious laborers might suffer (“they kill the dead instead of respecting them”).

## Bridge Maintenance

Stream Bridge Repair – Revised (20 cp, fixed rate)

Foundation Complications
	1.	Rising Creek / Storm Runoff
Sudden rain or snowmelt upstream swells Millrun Creek, washing away tools, scattering timbers, or threatening half-finished work.
• Choices: risk working in flood, haul materials to safety, improvise scaffolding.
	2.	Nesting Eagles
A mated pair of mountain eagles (larger, more dangerous than hawks) has taken to the bridge’s rafters. Fiercely territorial, they swoop at intruders.
• Choices: drive them off, relocate eggs, wait them out, or kill them (bad for reputation).
	3.	Mountain Weather
Freezing winds and sudden sleet make wet planks and ropes dangerously slick.
• Choices: halt work (lose time), improvise shelter, or risk accidents.

⸻

Replacement Complications (instead of settlers)

A. Old Construction Debris
	•	When tearing up rotten planks, the crew finds rusted tools and sealed crates from the Bulwark construction era wedged beneath the bridge.
	•	Some crates are waterlogged and fragile — but one may still contain salvageable iron fittings or half-rotted uniforms.
	•	Handling them risks splinters, collapsing supports, or offending the Authority if items are stolen.

Solutions:
	•	Deliver finds honestly (small reward, goodwill).
	•	Hide/sell them quietly.
	•	Use the extra timber and iron to patch up the bridge better than expected.

⸻

B. Military Oversight
	•	A pair of fortress inspectors (low-ranking officers) arrive “to check the work.”
	•	They’re impatient, condescending, and eager to fault the crew to cover their own laziness.
	•	They may insist the bridge be overbuilt or demand “proof” of effort, making the job harder.

Solutions:
	•	Impress them with solid work.
	•	Bribe or flatter them to overlook shortcuts.
	•	Ignore them (risking reports that hurt reputation).

⸻

C. Predatory Wildlife
	•	A half-starved mountain cat or pack of scavenging wolves prowls the creek, drawn by fish or carcasses in the water.
	•	They don’t attack outright but linger nearby, spooking anyone working.
	•	Tools, food, or supplies left unattended may be dragged off.

Solutions:
	•	Chase or scare them away with fire and noise.
	•	Leave food as a distraction.
	•	Kill or trap them (but risky for low-level workers).

⸻

D. Runoff Poison
	•	Millrun Creek sometimes carries black runoff from old quarries upstream. It coats wood in slick residue, weakening it.
	•	If used without cleaning, planks may warp and rot faster.
	•	Deciding whether to “do it quick” or “do it right” can matter.

Solutions:
	•	Clean planks properly (extra labor).
	•	Cut corners and hope nobody notices.
	•	Report problem to Authority (risking blame for delay).

⸻

Interconnections
	•	A swollen creek may dislodge the old crates, revealing construction debris.
	•	Predators may become bolder if bad weather strands the crew on-site.
	•	Inspectors may show up just as the eagles or floodwaters cause setbacks.

⸻

Outcomes
	•	Success: Bridge rebuilt, Authority pays full rate.
	•	Partial: Bridge stands but rushed work or accidents leave flaws; inspectors note poor quality.
	•	Failure: Collapse, injuries, or wasted materials; Authority refuses pay.
	•	Reputation Effects: Honest reporting and careful work = reliability. Cutting corners, wildlife deaths, or hiding salvage = suspicion but maybe quick coin.

⸻

👉 So instead of settlers, the complications now come from:
	•	The Bulwark’s past (hidden debris, ghosts of old work).
	•	The Authority itself (bureaucratic interference).
	•	Wildlife and environment (predators, poisoned runoff).

## Tower Demolition

Signal Tower Salvage – Revised (10 cp + salvage rights per tower)

Initial Setup

The Middle Bulwark Authority issues a contract: dismantle four obsolete wooden signal towers along the eastern ridge of Crow’s Peak. Workers may keep sound timber, but must return all iron fittings. The Authority doesn’t care about how the towers come down — safe dismantling, controlled collapse, or even felling them like trees.

Each tower stands on exposed crags, built during earlier frontier defense before the horn system replaced them. The ridge is high, cold, and wind-lashed.

⸻

Complications

1. Structural Instability
	•	Decayed beams and rotted platforms threaten collapse if climbed.
	•	A tower might fall in the wrong direction (into a work crew, or blocking the ridge path).
Solutions: rig ropes and wedges to control the fall, carefully climb with safety lines, dismantle from below, or deliberately topple the tower downslope.

2. Wildlife Nests
	•	Crows, ravens, or hawks nest in the rafters; some towers have owls or even goats sheltering beneath.
	•	Chicks and eggs create a moral dilemma if towers are felled wholesale.
	•	Swarms of angry birds may harass workers and steal food.
Solutions: scare off the adults, relocate nests, harvest eggs as food (practical but heartless), or carefully dismantle instead of felling.

3. Mountain Weather
	•	The ridge is prone to sudden gusts and storms rolling in from the north. Strong winds could topple an unsecured tower or throw workers off balance.
	•	Ice or sleet may coat ropes and beams, making them treacherous.
Solutions: wait out weather (costing time), double-up safety gear, improvise anchors, or gamble on speed.

4. Predators in the Ridge
	•	Mountain predators use the abandoned towers for shelter or vantage: wolves den under a platform, a lynx stalks the area, or a large eagle claims one as a perch.
	•	Predators might not immediately attack but become aggressive if disturbed.
Solutions: drive off, trap, or avoid them; sacrifice timber to smoke them out; coexist by working carefully around them.

5. Hidden History
	•	Inside one tower’s foundation, workers find a sealed supply cache (salted meat, rusted tools, or old orders from the Bulwark construction).
	•	Another has crude carvings or tally marks from sentries stationed there decades ago — signs of isolation and despair.
	•	Optional twist: bones beneath one, suggesting a sentry died forgotten.
Solutions: respect the find, bring items to the Authority (for small bonus pay), or keep them hidden.

6. Avalanche / Rockslide Risk
	•	Toppling a tower carelessly may trigger a landslide or avalanche down into the valley. This won’t affect anyone living (since it’s empty land), but it may bury timber or iron that the contractors must recover to get paid.
Solutions: plan the fall carefully, salvage from rubble, or report the loss (risking pay cuts).

⸻

Interconnections
	•	Felling towers quickly risks wildlife casualties and avalanches, but saves labor.
	•	Dismantling carefully takes longer, exposes workers to weather, and requires climbing rotten beams.
	•	Predators might take advantage of delays caused by bad weather or wildlife harassment.
	•	Discovering old caches or remains could provoke questions of conscience: loot or report?

⸻

Outcomes
	•	Efficient Felling: Towers all toppled quickly; Authority pays, but wildlife destroyed and salvage yield lower.
	•	Careful Dismantling: More time and risk, but more salvage and fewer moral compromises.
	•	Mixed Approach: Some towers felled, others dismantled. Outcomes vary by crew choices.
	•	Failure: Accidents, wasted materials, or unrecoverable fittings mean reduced pay and dangerous reputation (“unreliable amateurs”).

⸻

Tone & Theme

This job plays out like mountain logging crossed with high-stakes scaffolding work:
	•	Practical hazards (wind, rotten beams, rockslides).
	•	Moral choices (wildlife, forgotten remains).
	•	Naturalistic escalation — no need for bandits or rival workers, since the ridge itself is hostile and unforgiving.
